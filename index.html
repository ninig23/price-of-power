About the course

How power becomes a price

Why does electricity cost more at certain times, and how do those prices shape the way we produce and use energy? This course introduces the electricity grid and the markets that set the price of power.

Through real-world examples, you will explore how solar energy, battery storage and changing demand affect prices, and why the timing of electricity use matters for both consumers and the grid. The growing electricity needs of AI and data centers run through the course as a contemporary example.

Tuesdays combine accessible lectures with hands-on activities using real electricity data. Thursdays bring guest speakers from industry, government and academia who connect the ideas to real decisions. No background in engineering or economics is needed.

Try it · Week 1

The electricity market game

In the first week you play a power plant in a live classroom auction. Here is the idea in miniature: the cheapest plants run first, and the last plant needed to meet demand sets the price that every running plant is paid.

Simplified, illustrative costs and capacities. Real markets add transmission limits, reserves and bidding strategy.

    Questions

    Good to know

    Who can take this course?

    Advanced high-school students, undergraduates and graduate students in Stanford Summer Session, from any major.

    Do I need a background in engineering or economics?

    No. You need high-school algebra and a laptop. All activities use a spreadsheet, and the data is provided.

    How much work is it?

    About 3.5–4 hours a week on average, including class. You read something short (15–20 minutes) before each class. There is no weekly homework and there are no exams; the final project takes place in the second half.

    What are the reading check-ins?

    At the start of every class you scan a QR code and answer two or three questions about the reading. Answers are graded on completion, not correctness. They start the discussion.

    When does it meet?

    Tuesdays and Thursdays, 75 minutes each, for eight weeks. The proposed time is 1:30–2:45 PM. Final dates and times are set by Stanford Summer Session.

    Who are the guest speakers?

    Practitioners from grid operations, energy planning, government, industry and Stanford are being invited. None are confirmed yet; speakers will be announced before the session.

    Is there a graduate version?

    Graduate students may take the course under a separate course number with an extended written analysis of about five pages, including original data analysis and a policy recommendation.

    Can I use AI tools?

    Yes, to help understand concepts or check spreadsheet formulas. Your analysis and writing must be your own, and you note any AI use in one sentence at the end of your work.
